Most bad freelance experiences are decided before any code is written. They happen because the client and the developer both had a different picture of the project in their heads, neither of them wrote it down, and they discovered the gap six weeks in.

This guide is written for the client side. I have been on the other end of these conversations, and the pattern is consistent enough to be predictable.

Before you write a job post

The most useful thing you can do happens before you contact anyone: write the project down.

Not a feature list. A description of what the software does, in sentences.

“Customers search for available rooms for two dates, we hold the room for ten minutes while they pay, we take a card payment, and they get an email confirmation. Staff see the same bookings in an admin area and can cancel them.”

That paragraph is worth more than fifty bullet points, because it exposes the real complexity. It hints at availability logic, a timeout, payment integration, transactional email and an authorisation model — all things a fifty-item feature list would have hidden under vague words like “calendar” and “payments”.

Then do the highest-leverage exercise available: decide what is not in version one. Every feature you defer is money and weeks saved. Most projects succeed because of what they left out, not because of how well they were built.

What a good job post contains

  • What the product does and who uses it
  • The existing stack, if there is one
  • The engagement model: fixed scope, hourly, or ongoing
  • The timeline you have in mind, and whether it is fixed or flexible
  • The time zone overlap you can genuinely work with
  • How you want to communicate: calls, async written updates, or both
  • What “done” looks like

Notice what is not on the list: your budget, unless you have a real range and are comfortable stating it. Filtering candidates on budget before knowing the scope is how good developers get screened out of projects they would have done well.

What to ask in the first conversation

Most hiring mistakes are made in a thirty-minute call. These are the questions that surface the important information.

“Walk me through something you built that is similar.” Listen for specifics, not adjectives. “I built a booking system” tells you nothing. “We had a double-booking problem under load, so I moved availability checks into a transaction with a row lock” tells you they have dealt with a real system.

“How do you decide what is in scope?” A good answer involves asking you questions and pushing back on parts of the request. A bad answer is “whatever you want.” If they never push back, they will not push back on a deadline either.

“What does your testing look like?” Any credible answer includes automated tests. “I test manually” is a complete answer and it should end the conversation for anything that will be maintained.

“Who owns the code and the accounts?” Everything — the repository, the hosting, the domain, the database, the third-party accounts. Under your organisation, from the first commit. If a developer wants to keep access to your infrastructure after the project ends, that is a serious red flag.

“What happens if the project needs to change halfway through?” You want to hear about a change-order process, not “we would just handle it.” Scope changes are normal. Undocumented scope changes are how projects go badly.

“Can you give me a reference or show me the live product?” Every serious freelancer can. If they cannot, ask why.

Red flags

Guaranteed timelines with undefined scope. Anyone who can promise a date before understanding the project is guessing.

No questions about your requirements. A developer who does not ask what the users actually do has not thought about the product.

Hourly with no estimate. Hourly billing with no scope and no estimate has no ceiling. If they will not give you a range, they have not thought about the work.

Pressure to start immediately. “I have capacity this week, can we sign today” is a sales tactic. Good developers are busy and will not be destabilised by you not deciding today.

Everything is a full-time commitment. Be suspicious of anyone who cannot describe what else they are working on. A freelancer with no other clients is either between projects or telling you something untrue.

Refusing to break the work into milestones. All-at-once payment for a multi-month project puts all the risk on you and removes any leverage to finish. Milestones are not distrust; they are the standard.

Rates

For a competent freelance Ruby on Rails developer in 2026, expect roughly:

  • $60–$100/hour for a strong generalist
  • $100–$150/hour for someone with specific Hotwire Native, Rails performance or security expertise
  • $30–$60/hour for developers earlier in their careers or in lower-cost markets

Two warnings.

First, a rate that is dramatically below the market is not a bargain. It usually means less experience, or a plan to raise the price mid-project once you are dependent.

Second, the cheapest quote is rarely the cheapest project. I have watched clients pay $25/hour, then spend forty hours on a codebase nobody else wanted to maintain. The hourly saving evaporated and the schedule slipped anyway.

For fixed-scope work, judge on the total price and the quality of the scope, not the implied rate.

Contracts: what should be in them

You do not need a lawyer for a $20,000 project. You do need a written document covering:

  • Scope — what is being built, specifically enough that both of you would recognise it
  • Deliverables — what you receive and when
  • Milestones and payment — amounts and dates, with a schedule
  • Revision policy — how many rounds of changes per milestone, and how additional work is priced
  • Change process — how scope changes are agreed and billed
  • Code ownership — you own everything, from the first commit
  • Confidentiality — mutual, and it should survive the end of the project
  • Termination — how either side exits, and what happens to work in progress

A shared document with a scope, a price and a date is dramatically better than no document. Legal review is worth it for contracts above roughly $50,000 or where you are dealing with something unusual.

The engagement models

Fixed scope, fixed price. The best default once the requirements are clear. Aligns incentives and gives you a predictable number. Requires both sides to have genuinely agreed the scope first.

Hourly. Right for audits, maintenance, exploratory work, and anything where you do not yet know what the answer is. Wrong for building something whose scope you have not thought through.

Ongoing retainer. A block of hours each month. Good for teams shipping continuously who want steady capacity without a hiring cycle. Make sure the terms let you stop.

Consultation first. A short, fixed-fee engagement that produces a written assessment before any implementation. It costs you a few thousand dollars, and it is the cheapest risk reduction available. You keep the document whether or not you continue.

How to run the work once you have hired someone

Use a repository with proper history from day one. Commits, branches, pull requests. If the code is in a zip file, that is already a bad sign.

Get written weekly updates. Short, factual, what shipped, what is blocked, what is next. This catches a drifting schedule in week two rather than week eight.

Make decisions quickly. The most common cause of delay is client-side indecision, not technical difficulty. If you can decide in a day what would take a week to deliberate, say so and hold yourself to it.

Test at the end of each milestone, not at the end of the project. Discovering a misunderstanding in week two costs a conversation. Discovering it in week ten costs a rewrite.

Review the code. You are paying for a codebase someone else will maintain. If you have a technical stakeholder, they should read some of it. If nobody on your side can read it, that is worth knowing early.

What a good freelance relationship looks like

The best engagements I have had share a few things: the scope was written down, the client decided quickly, we talked weekly, and nobody was surprised. Problems surfaced early and were treated as information rather than as blame.
