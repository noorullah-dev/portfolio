“How much will my app cost?” is the question I am asked most, and the honest answer is more useful than the one clients usually want to hear: it depends almost entirely on scope, and the range for any given project is wide.

What I can do is give you real numbers from real projects, explain what drives them, and flag the costs people forget to budget for. Those forgotten costs are frequently larger than the development itself.

Realistic ranges by project type

These are 2026 figures for a competent team, based on what I charge and what I see in the market.

Project typeTypical costTimeline

The number that surprises most people is the first one. A good marketing site is not a $40,000 project, and if you are being quoted that, ask what is included.

What actually drives the cost

Number of distinct screens

Not pages — screens. A booking platform with a search page has six to ten distinct states: loading, no results, partial results, availability conflict, price breakdown, payment, confirmation, error. Each one is design, build, test and edge-case work.

This is why “it’s just a booking form” is never just a booking form.

Integrations

Every third-party service is a project in miniature. Payments, email, SMS, maps, calendar sync, invoicing, accounting, a shipping API, an identity provider. Budget two to five days per straightforward integration, and considerably more for anything with an awkward API or a webhooks-that-fire-at-3am problem.

Integrations are also where scope quietly grows. One integration often reveals three requirements you did not know about.

Authentication and roles

“Users can log in” becomes “users log in, reset passwords, verify email, have roles, get invited by an admin, see different navigation per role, and lose access when suspended” before the first sprint ends. Budget for it up front.

Content and how it is managed

Who updates the text? If it is your developer, you have saved money. If it is a marketing manager on day one, you have bought a CMS, which is a real project of its own.

Quality expectations

This is the one clients rarely think about. There is a large gap between software that works in a demo and software that handles real users: error states, validation, rate limiting, backups, monitoring, email deliverability, accessibility, and the hundred small decisions that only matter once traffic arrives.

You are paying for this whether you budget for it or not. The only question is whether you pay for it deliberately or pay for it twice.

The costs people forget

This is the part that turns a $30,000 project into a $60,000 one. In rough order of how often they surprise people:

Hosting and infrastructure. $20 to $500 per month for a small application, more as traffic grows. Managed Postgres, object storage, a background job worker, error tracking, uptime monitoring. Not large, but it is never zero and it never stops.

Domain, email and transactional email. $15–$200 per year. Transactional email is the one that gets missed — you need a provider, and you need to authenticate your sending domain or your password reset emails will be marked as spam.

Payment processing. Stripe and similar charge roughly 2.9% plus $0.30 per transaction. Your margin needs to absorb this.

App store fees. 15–30% of in-app revenue, depending on platform and program. Apple and Google take a larger cut than web payments, and this materially changes your unit economics.

Maintenance. 15–25% of development cost per year after launch. This is not optional. Rails and its dependencies need security updates, the Ruby version needs moving, the database needs upgrading, and something will break at 2am.

Content. Copywriting, photography, product photography, video. Frequently 10–20% of the total project cost, and almost never in the original budget.

Legal and compliance. Terms of service, privacy policy, and — if you take payments or handle personal data across jurisdictions — real legal advice. A privacy policy generator is not legal advice.

Your own time. The most consistently underestimated cost. You will be required for decisions, content, testing and feedback. Founders regularly budget zero for this and then the project stalls for three weeks while they are busy with their actual job.

How to get an accurate quote

You cannot get an accurate quote from a description. You can get an estimate, and then narrow it. Here is the process that works:

Write down what the software does, in sentences, not features. “A user can search for available rooms between two dates, hold a room for ten minutes while they pay, and receive a confirmation by email.” That sentence is worth more than a fifty-item feature list, because it exposes the real complexity.

Decide what is not in version one. This is the highest-leverage thing you can do before contacting anyone. Every feature you defer is directly money and time saved. Most projects succeed because of what they left out.

Get a written scope, not a verbal estimate. Any competent developer will produce a document listing deliverables, a fixed price and a delivery date. If you cannot get that, they are guessing, and so are you.

Ask what they are assuming. A quote that does not state its assumptions is not a quote. “This assumes existing accounts system, no payment processing, content supplied by you” tells you far more than the number.

Reserve 20%. Not padding — the reality that the first version of anything surfaces requirements nobody anticipated. A project that delivers exactly the agreed scope with zero contingency is a project that hit a problem and someone absorbed it.

On hourly versus fixed price

Hourly makes sense for undefined work: an audit, ongoing maintenance, an emergency, exploratory work where you genuinely do not know what the answer is yet.

Fixed price makes sense once the scope is agreed. It aligns incentives — the developer is motivated to build it efficiently, and you have a predictable number.

The failure mode is fixed price on undefined work. The developer is now carrying the risk of scope you have not thought of, and the predictable outcome is either a change-order conversation or an under-built product.

What I would tell a friend

If you have $20,000 and a business idea, spend it on the smallest thing that tests whether anyone wants it. Not a beautiful version of your full vision — the crudest possible version that solves the one problem that matters.

Most projects fail because nobody wanted what was built, not because the code was not good enough. Finding that out for $8,000 is much better than finding it out for $80,000.
